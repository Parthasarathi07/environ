# Deck transcript

Read this aloud once before presenting; the phrasing is deliberately plain.

### Slide 1 — Title

> Does a high ESG score pay? I spent a term hearing two different answers to that question, so I built the analysis that separates them. On 160 firms / 108 months of data: no return spread you can detect, a risk gradient you can, and the famous ESG underperformance turns out to be a sector bet.

### Slide 2 — Why

> The confusion is between two claims. Alpha says better-run firms are underpriced and will outperform. Risk says better-run firms fall less when things go wrong. If it's alpha you buy the leaders. If it's risk you're selling a different product at a different price.

### Slide 3 — Method

> Every month I rank firms into five equal buckets on their published ESG score and hold them for a month. Then I do it again after subtracting what each firm's own sector did that month. And I cluster standard errors by firm, because each firm appears about a hundred times.

### Slide 4 — Result 1

> Taken at face value, top minus bottom is -2.0% a year. Sector-neutral, -0.8% a year, t of -0.5. Two bars, same portfolios, one is a headline and one is an answer.

### Slide 5 — Result 2

> Where the signal lives is risk. Volatility falls from 15.9 to 12.4 percent, downside capture from 119 to 83, and the worst drawdown improves -6.4 points. It's 3/4 steps falling steps in the right direction, not a perfect line — I say that because the middle buckets are where a chart like this gets checked.

### Slide 6 — Result 3

> This chart is the whole debate. Look at 2022: on that year the naive bar is -20.4% and the sector-neutral bar is +1.6%. Same stocks, same months.

### Slide 7 — Result 4

> So I decomposed it. In 2022, -22.6 points came from which sectors each book held, +2.3 from stock-picking inside sectors. That sums to the headline by construction. Energy was 81 percent up that year and it is, sensibly, in the bottom ESG quintile — that is not an ESG failure, that is an energy call.

### Slide 8 — Result 5

> The one significant return result is momentum, not the level: +26.3 basis points a month per standard deviation of rating improvement, t of +4.94. Buy the improvers, not the leaders. With the caveat that momentum decays and this is before turnover.

### Slide 9 — Robustness

> Four specifications, and the level coefficient wanders every time I add a control. That is not a weakness of my analysis, it's the central fact of the literature — and the honest thing is to show the intervals rather than the point estimate.

### Slide 10 — Honesty test

> I built a switch that sets the assumed ESG effects to zero and regenerates the panel. The verdict text rewrites itself to a null. Any study that can't fail a test like that isn't a study.

### Slide 11 — Limits

> Synthetic demo data, fixed universe, one rater, no costs, correlation not causation. The fixed universe actually flatters the number I'm criticising, so my conclusion is conservative in that one direction.

### Slide 12 — Recommendation

> Sell ESG as downside control, tilt on rating improvement, and never show an ESG return figure without the sector-neutral one next to it. For a CFO: the ESG case is cost of capital, not growth.
